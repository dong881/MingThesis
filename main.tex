\section{Introduction}
\subsection{Background and Motivation}

\textcolor{blue}{With the evolution of 5G and the Open Radio Access Network (O-RAN), architectures based on functional splits have become the key to improving network flexibility and reducing deployment costs. Currently, the most mainstream implementation promoted by the O-RAN Alliance is Split Option 7.2 (Split~7.2). Because Split~7.2 divides the Physical (PHY) layer into high and low parts and transmits continuous in-phase and quadrature (IQ) samples, it imposes extremely high requirements on the fronthaul network.}

\textcolor{blue}{Compared to Split~7.2, the 3rd Generation Partnership Project (3GPP) Split Option 6 (Split~6), which separates the network at the Media Access Control (MAC) and PHY layers, offers a more flexible alternative. First, Split~7.2 relies on hardware-based synchronization protocols, such as IEEE 1588 Precision Time Protocol (PTP) and Synchronous Ethernet (SyncE), to achieve nanosecond-level phase and time synchronization. Its timing window requirement is typically strict, often needing to be less than 100~\textmu s. In contrast, the nFAPI (Network Functional Application Platform Interface) protocol, which is a prominent implementation of Split~6, employs a message-based synchronization mechanism. Through its proprietary P7 Node Sync procedure, nFAPI achieves slot-level synchronization (millisecond-level) and provides a highly flexible timing window ranging from 0 to 30,000~\textmu s. Furthermore, in terms of payload, Split~6 transports packetized MAC Protocol Data Units (PDUs) rather than continuous IQ samples. These characteristics allow Split~6 to demonstrate better adaptability in non-ideal fronthaul network environments where high jitter from operating system (OS) scheduling and network delivery is present.}

\textcolor{blue}{This paper chooses Split~6 as the primary research subject due to its significant deployment benefits. As indicated by P\'{e}rez-Romero et al. \cite{10186379}, Split~6 effectively reduces the traffic burden on the fronthaul network. Compared to low-layer split schemes that transmit frequency-domain signals, its bandwidth requirement is significantly lower. In addition, Khan et al. \cite{10014970} demonstrate that for the deployment of sub-6 GHz small cell networks, Split~6 can maintain robust transmission performance over non-ideal fronthaul connections, thereby achieving an optimal cost-revenue trade-off.}

\textcolor{blue}{Deploying the VNF and PNF on different machines introduces significant challenges. Because the MAC scheduler is separated from the PHY layer, strict slot synchronization is required between these distributed components. However, different traffic loads and non-ideal network deployments introduce unpredictable, non-deterministic latency. Consequently, the VNF cannot reliably transmit data exactly at the slot boundary, and existing open-source solutions suffer from synchronization failures when encountering unpredictable latency from routers and switches. This paper defines these latency and synchronization problems under non-ideal fronthaul networks and proposes a dynamic trigger scheduler adjustment algorithm. A detailed analysis of this non-deterministic latency and the corresponding verification of these problems will be presented in Scenario I of the numerical results section.}

\section{System Model/Architecture}

\textcolor{blue}{Before diving into the system details, this study establishes a prerequisite assumption: since there is currently a lack of O-RAN Radio Units (O-RUs) in the market that natively support Split~6 nFAPI, the system adopts a two-stage split architecture. First, it communicates via the Split~6 interface, and then translates to the Split~7.2x standard to connect to the remote radio unit.}

\subsection{End-to-End Architecture}
\textcolor{blue}{This system establishes a complete 5G end-to-end communication link, extending from the core network down to the user equipment:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Core Network (CN):} Responsible for routing and authenticating user data.
\item \textbf{O-RAN Central Unit (O-CU):} Handles higher-layer protocol stacks (such as RRC and PDCP) and connects with distributed units via the F1 interface.
\item \textbf{O-RAN Distributed Unit (O-DU):} To optimize performance, the system splits the O-DU into an O-DU High (running on a Virtualized Network Function, VNF machine) and an O-DU Low (running on a Physical Network Function, PNF machine).
\item \textbf{Switch:} A switch is located between the VNF and PNF. This connection uses the nFAPI (Network Functional Application Platform Interface) protocol, but due to the network environment, non-deterministic latency is generated.
\item \textbf{O-RAN Radio Unit (O-RU):} Responsible for low-level physical layer (Low PHY) processing and RF transmission.
\item \textbf{COTS UE:} Commercial Off-The-Shelf User Equipment.
\end{itemize}}

\subsection{O-DU High: MAC and Delay Manager}
\textcolor{blue}{Within the Media Access Control (MAC) layer of the O-DU High, the core components include the Scheduler and the nFAPI Delay Manager:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Scheduler:} Responsible for allocating radio resources (such as time-frequency resource blocks) and determining the priority of data transmission.
\item \textbf{nFAPI Delay Manager:} This is the crucial control unit of this architecture. Because unstable latency exists in the switch between the VNF and PNF, the Delay Manager receives feedback information from the lower physical layer and dynamically triggers the scheduler based on the current network jitter. This ensures that the scheduling instructions (\texttt{Sched data}) are generated in a timely manner.
\end{itemize}}

\subsection{O-DU Low: High PHY and Timing Window}
\textcolor{blue}{The O-DU Low contains the higher-level processing of the physical layer (High PHY). At this level, the most critical mechanism is the nFAPI PNF Timing Window:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Timing Window:} This is a predefined time range. When the scheduling message (\texttt{DL\_TTI.request}) sent by the O-DU High arrives, it must accurately fall within this window for the High PHY to process the subframe data correctly.
\item \textbf{Timing Info:} The High PHY continuously monitors the arrival time of the messages and sends this timing information back to the Delay Manager in the MAC layer. This forms a closed-loop feedback control system, which compensates for the transmission latency between the VNF and PNF machines.
\end{itemize}}

\subsection{Key Validation Metrics}
\textcolor{blue}{To evaluate the stability and real-time performance of the system, this study defines the following three key validation metrics, where RTT stands for Round-Trip Time:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Ping RTT ($T_7 - T_1$):} Measures the end-to-end round-trip latency at the application layer, representing the complete time from when data is sent from the core network to when the return acknowledgment is received.
\item \textbf{HARQ RTT ($T_6 - T_2$):} Measures the Hybrid Automatic Repeat Request (HARQ) loop delay at the MAC layer, reflecting the feedback efficiency of the link layer.
\item \textbf{VNF-PNF Downlink Latency ($T_3 - T_2$):} Specifically measures the one-way downlink latency from the virtualized machine to the physical machine, used to analyze the delay impact caused by the switch.
\end{itemize}}

\subsection{Scenario I: Analysis of Non-Deterministic Latency}

\textcolor{blue}{Deploying the VNF and PNF on different machines introduces significant challenges.
Because the MAC scheduler is separated from the PHY layer, strict slot synchronization is required between these distributed components.
However, even when deployed in the exact same network environment, different traffic loads directly impact the latency.
Figure~\ref{fig:Scheduler-to-PNF-latency} illustrates the latency between the VNF scheduler and the PNF under varying traffic loads.
As traffic increases, three factors heighten the overall delay: the scheduler requires more processing time, the data packing time lengthens, and the VNF-to-PNF One-Way Delay (OWD) is affected.
Consequently, the VNF cannot reliably transmit data exactly at the slot boundary.
Instead, it must dynamically advance its scheduling point to compensate for these varying delays and ensure packets arrive at the PNF on time.}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.8\columnwidth]{figures/Scheduler-to-PNF-latency.png}
  \caption{Observation of latency between VNF scheduler and PNF under different traffic loads.}
  \label{fig:Scheduler-to-PNF-latency}
\end{figure}

\textcolor{blue}{To lower deployment costs, operators often want to deploy Split-6 nFAPI gNBs over non-ideal networks.
However, beyond traffic load variations, these non-ideal networks introduce additional, unpredictable latency.
Figure~\ref{fig:Non-Ideal-Latency} shows the latency jitter caused by intermediate routers and switches in a non-ideal deployment.
Existing open-source solutions, such as OpenAirInterface (OAI), provide basic nFAPI support but were not designed to fully comply with the nFAPI specification's delay management procedures \cite{nfapi_spec}.
Therefore, they suffer from synchronization failures when encountering unpredictable latency from routers and switches.
To address these challenges, this paper proposes a dynamic trigger scheduler adjustment algorithm that fully complies with the specification.
Through systematic experiments, we verify the effectiveness of this dynamic adjustment and its benefits to overall network reliability.}
