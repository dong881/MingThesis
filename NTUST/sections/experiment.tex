\chapter{Experimental Results}
\label{sec:experiment}

This study conducts experiments in an OpenAirInterface (OAI) nFAPI end-to-end environment to verify the proposed delay management method. To ensure the statistical reliability of the evaluation, each throughput value reported represents the average of 180 data points (3 independent trials $\times$ 60 samples per trial).

To systematically evaluate the performance of our proposed closed-loop synchronization and delay-management algorithms, the experimental results are designed to answer the following research questions:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{RQ1:} Does the EWMA-based filtering effectively stabilize the noisy PNF Timing Info feedback under transport network jitter?
\item \textbf{RQ2:} Can the adaptive slots ahead method maintain stable throughput while minimizing unnecessary MAC HARQ RTT compared with static baselines?
\item \textbf{RQ3:} Can the proposed closed-loop delay management preserve end-to-end synchronization stability and throughput under severe network congestion and dynamic jitter?
\item \textbf{RQ4:} Does the adaptive timing-control loop remain stable and effective when the VNF-PNF transport path traverses a realistic multi-hop deployment?
\end{itemize}

The rest of this chapter is organized as follows: Section~\ref{subsec:experimental_scenario} outlines the hardware, software, and the automated rApp evaluation framework, defining both Experimental Scenario I and Experimental Scenario II. Section~\ref{subsec:scenario_1} addresses RQ1 under Experimental~1. Section~\ref{subsec:scenario_2} addresses RQ2 under Experimental~2. Section~\ref{subsec:scenario_3} addresses RQ3 under Experimental~3. Finally, Section~\ref{subsec:scenario_4} addresses RQ4 under Experimental~4.

\section{Experimental Scenario}
\label{subsec:experimental_scenario}

To evaluate the performance of our proposed timing adjustment framework under different environments, we design two topologies:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Experimental Scenario I (Controlled Single-Switch environment):} A standard testbed where the VNF and PNF are connected directly via a single commercial Ethernet switch. This controlled setup is used to analyze EWMA filtering, benchmark throughput-latency trade-offs against static baselines, and evaluate robustness under Linux-TC-injected delay and jitter.
\item \textbf{Experimental Scenario II (Multi-Switch/Router Campus Deployment):} A realistic multi-hop campus network topology where the VNF and PNF are physically disaggregated across different locations. As shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:scenario2_topology}, the connection traverses $3$ commercial switches and $3$ commercial routers, introducing realistic transport delays and route-dependent jitter.
\end{itemize}

To ensure consistent data quality for automated measurement and analysis, this study developed an automated evaluation rApp running on the Non-Real-Time RAN Intelligent Controller (Non-RT RIC) within the O-RAN Service Management and Orchestration (SMO) framework \cite{O-RAN.arch, O-RAN.rapp}, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:rapp_architecture}. This rApp automatically starts the VNF and PNF. Once the gNB is successfully established, the rApp automatically connects to the control PC via SSH, toggles the UE's airplane mode using the Android Debug Bridge (ADB) \cite{ADB}, and then launches an iPerf \cite{iperf3} server on the Core Network server via SSH. Subsequently, it automatically executes a series of predefined iPerf test cases. After that, the rApp collects all log files via O1 SFTP and uses Python scripts to automatically plot charts for subsequent data analysis. During the experiments, we also used tcpdump \cite{tcpdump} for packet sniffing, and utilized Linux PTP \cite{LinuxPTP311} and TSN time synchronization mechanisms \cite{tsn-timesync} to ensure baseline timing among network nodes, while underlying data transmission relied on DPDK \cite{DPDK20119} for packet acceleration. A key capability of this framework is the ability to automate network delay simulations on the MAC feedback link ($T_5-T_4$) and fronthaul link ($T_2-T_1$) using Linux Traffic Control (TC), which is crucial for verifying the stability of the disaggregated architecture under non-deterministic latency.

\subsection{Thorough System-Level Integration and Validation}
\label{subsec:software_validation}

To prove the stability, practical deployability, and compatibility of the proposed timing control and nFAPI modifications, our software implementation was submitted and successfully merged into the official upstream OpenAirInterface (OAI) repository \cite{nikaein2014openairinterface}. Beyond our localized end-to-end evaluation using the rApp framework, the proposed solution went through thorough testing in the official OAI Continuous Integration and Continuous Deployment (CI/CD) pipeline \cite{oai_gitlab_ci}. This automated pipeline executes multi-platform builds and physical layer regression suites to ensure production-grade software quality.

In the software compilation and image deployment stage, the proposed implementation demonstrates high portability across heterogeneous hardware architectures. As compiled in Table~\ref{tab:oai_cicd_build}, the cross-platform builds successfully passed on standard x86 servers, ARM64-based edge servers, and NVIDIA Jetson edge AI platforms under both Ubuntu and enterprise-grade Red Hat Enterprise Linux (RHEL) \cite{redhat_rhel} environments. These results confirm that our proposed timing control is independent of specific hardware acceleration cards, allowing seamless deployment on general-purpose Commercial Off-The-Shelf (COTS) cloud infrastructure.

\section{Experimental 3: Stability under Network Congestion}
\label{subsec:scenario_3}

To test the system's stability, we utilized Linux TC to inject artificial network congestion into the $T_2-T_1$ and $T_5-T_4$ segments, altering the One-Way Delay (OWD) and jitter. As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:exp3a}, when the delay exceeds the fixed timing window, the static configuration experiences a severe drop in throughput and frequent connection resets. In contrast, the proposed adaptive timing control dynamically expands the slots ahead ($S_{ahead}$). This expansion allows the MAC scheduler to trigger messages in advance, ensuring that messages can still arrive at the PNF within the valid timing window despite the increased transmission delay. Therefore, even at congestion levels that would previously cause complete synchronization failure, the system can maintain stable throughput.

To further evaluate the impact of unpredictable network jitter between the VNF and PNF, we perform a second experiment using Linux TC to simulate random jitter on the fronthaul link. In this test, we run iPerf UDP traffic with an offered load of 1 Gbps to fully saturate the downlink transmission. We measure the resulting downlink (DL) receiver (RX) throughput at the UE, comparing different fixed slot ahead configurations against our proposed adaptive timing mechanism. As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:exp3b}, when network jitter is high and unpredictable, fixed slot ahead settings cannot handle the timing variations, leading to packet losses, sync failures, and a major drop in throughput. On the other hand, the proposed adaptive method dynamically adjusts the scheduling advance, keeping the transmission timing aligned. As a result, the UE achieves and maintains stable and high DL RX throughput even under severe jitter conditions.

\section{Experimental 4: Stability under Multiple Switches and Routers}
\label{subsec:scenario_4}

To evaluate whether the proposed adaptive timing-control mechanism remains effective beyond the controlled single-switch environment, we deploy the VNF and PNF across the multi-hop campus network in Experimental Scenario II. As shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:scenario2_topology}, the VNF-side components, including Open5GS, O-CU, and O-DU High, are placed at a remote computing site, while the O-DU Low, O-RU, and COTS UE remain at the radio-side site. The connection between the two sites traverses $3$ commercial switches and $3$ commercial routers, introducing realistic non-deterministic transport delay and route-dependent jitter. This scenario therefore evaluates the practical robustness of the proposed delay-management loop under a more complex deployment environment.

Fig.~\ref{fig:exp4a} shows the UE downlink RX throughput (the blue dashed line) and the ping latency (the purple solid line) over a continuous timeline during which different levels of offered load are injected. The proposed method successfully completes the entire transmission process under varying offered loads.

Furthermore, the figure illustrates that when the network is idle between load steps, the baseline ping latency is low and stable at around 0.3 to 0.4 ms. As the injected load increases, the ping latency fluctuates and rises up to approximately 2.0 ms due to growing queueing delay across the multi-hop path. Despite this severe and dynamic jitter, the proposed method dynamically adapts the slots ahead to prevent late packet arrivals, thereby maintaining stable data transmission without connection drops. These results confirm the feasibility of deploying the proposed method across the campus-wide, multi-hop network environment.
